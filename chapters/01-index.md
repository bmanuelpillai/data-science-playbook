# My Philosophy of Coding as a Researcher

*Coding as a Tool for Research*

## The Motivation to Learn to Code as a Researcher

### The Start Is The Hardest Part

When you first start to program, it can feel really overwhelming. You aren't comfortable with the language, and you have to look up everything before you can do what you want. You'll see more experienced programmers doing things a lot faster and in ways you've never seen before. It can be discouraging, but rest assured this will be the hardest part of your programming journey. Once you gain experience and confidence as a programmer, you will feel like you can tackle any problem.

## The Doors Coding Opens for Researchers

Developing your coding skills opens a lot of doors when you go to analyze your data. If you have lots of data, you probably will not be able to run your analysis on your local computer due to hardware constraints. Learning the basics of computing clusters or cloud computing will allow you to tackle this problem, which is especially important for computationally demanding methods such as Bayesian inference.

## Why Coding is Fun

Coming from a STEM background, one of my favorite parts about programming is how you get instant feedback from running your code. Either your code runs or you get an error message. Every time your code runs, you get a feeling of accomplishment that drives you to overcome the next bug. Every time you squash a bug, you improve your ability to diagnose and fix the next one.

I also love the community of developers and researchers who support you through the material they share online. You can look at other people's projects or online tutorials for inspiration. I highly recommend looking for course websites that professors post online, as they are tailored for researchers and often include homework assignments to reinforce your learning. Most importantly, you learn by doing, so pick projects you enjoy.

## Forest vs. Trees

Don't miss the forest for the trees. I think coding is especially fun for scientists because it isn't our main job. We use code as a tool to answer the questions that interest us; our goal is not to write the most optimized or well-written. You may become really good at coding as a byproduct of tackling your research questions, but coding is just a means to an end. Write code first, then optimize it later if needed. That is not to say your code shouldn't be readable -- it should still be well documented so that others can work on it too.

I include a lot of tools that make coding easier and more fun, but when you are first starting, focus on getting the basics down. That comes with time and practice. I recommend following one course and then spending most of your time on projects.
